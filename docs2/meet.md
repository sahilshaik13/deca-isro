### Summary

Team aligned on architecture and simulation strategy with mentors for the upcoming hackathon performance and success.  
<br/>**Technical architecture and models**  
The system utilizes containerized local servers with Prometheus pipelines and machine learning models for predictive fault analysis. Explainability relies on a Retrieval Augmented Generation framework alongside Long Short Term Memory networks.  
<br/>**Quality of service integration**  
Mentors advised incorporating Quality of Service and Service Level Agreement parameters to distinguish high-priority traffic during congestion. This ensures the prediction model identifies specific rogue nodes affecting critical customer availability — including **CE↔CE SLA policy conflict** (lower-SLA burst harming higher-SLA CE) and **per-CE bandwidth anomalies** (quiet 2–3 Mbps → sudden ~20 Mbps). The demo target is a **working NOC**: many CEs feeding one hub, multiple operators handling predictive alerts.  
<br/>**Shift to virtualized simulation**  
The team decided to adopt a Graphical Network Simulator 3 virtualized environment with 15 to 20 nodes. This shift prioritizes real-time predictability over physical hardware dependencies.

### Decisions

Aligned

- **Integration of SLA and QoS** The project scope is expanded to include network segregation based on Service Level Agreements (SLAs) and Quality of Service (QoS) classes.
- **Prioritization of fault predictability** The project development strategy is refocused to prioritize network fault prediction capabilities over explanability and GUI features.
- **Simple clickable faults (mentor must-show)** Dashboard buttons for short, single faults (rain fade, CPU, BGP, CE SLA conflict, loss). Click → live inject → telemetry moves → Decide predicts → Approve. Keep the long Phases 0–6 timeline secondary.
- **Adoption of GNS3 simulation** The simulation methodology is updated to incorporate a GNS3-based 15-20 node network model to facilitate more effective fault injection testing.
- **CE↔CE SLA policy conflict (mentor must-show)** When a lower-SLA / scavenger CE bursts, it must not silently starve a higher-SLA CE. NOC must surface **policy conflict**: who is the rogue CE, who is the victim, which CoS/SLA is at risk, and what action protects the critical class (preempt / throttle / steer).
- **CE bandwidth anomaly / “security-shaped” NOC use-case** Mentors gave the Income-Tax-style CE example: quiet edge **2–3 Mbps** baseline suddenly **~15–20 Mbps**. Treat as **network anomaly for uptime** (rogue/abusive consumer, misconfig, or attack-shaped surge) — predict breach, name the CE, protect higher SLA — **not** a full security product (IDS/malware). Same family: optical TX/RX budget collapse, sudden util spike on one CE while peers stay quiet.
- **NOC is the product** Many CEs attach toward **one operational hub**; a single NOC (with **multiple operators**) watches the fleet, gets predictive alerts, and Approve/Rejects remediation. Demo must look like operators running a NOC, not a laptop one-off script.

### Details

- **Meeting Opening and Purpose**: The meeting was initiated by Pravin Choudhary, who introduced a colleague, Mithilesh, to evaluate the team's project direction. Pravin Choudhary outlined that the core objective of the session was to understand how the team intends to simulate, inject, and predict network faults within their dataset during the upcoming hackathon finale.
- **Network Simulation Infrastructure**: Mohammed Shaik Sahil presented the technical architecture, detailing the use of Iperf for network simulation and Cisco T-Rex for digital signature simulation. The hardware architecture is built upon three physical Raspberry Pi servers, which run a containerized network setup locally to maintain a completely offline, air-gapped system. Mohammed Shaik Sahil specified that the network topology is distributed across four customer edges (NRSC Hyderabad, Mauritius, Ahmedabad, and MFC Hassan), two provider edges, and two core edges. The setup achieves network separation via VPN segmentation, operating with a mission-critical line, a backup line, and a direct third line connection in case both primary lines fail, alongside an 80 underlay backup for congested paths.
- **Data Pipeline and Latency Management**: Mohammed Shaik Sahil explained that the system achieves minimal latency by streaming probe data (including Telegraph, OPF, and IPSec encryption metrics) directly from the network paths into a Prometheus database using Kafka streams. The pipeline collects data at a frequency of 1 Hz (once per second). Mohammed Shaik Sahil noted that the machine learning model is designed to operate within millisecond thresholds, featuring a fallback mechanism if network-wide latency reaches 2 seconds, thereby staying on pace with real-time network speeds.
- **Machine Learning and Local LLM Architecture**: Mohammed Shaik Sahil confirmed that the entire machine learning stack, notebooks, and rack system run offline locally on laptops. For the AI components, the team utilizes a 3-billion parameter Phi-3 model for the Large Language Model (LLM) explainer tasks and Nomic Embed Text for the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework. To enable network architecture understanding and accurate IP querying, the team fed approximately six technical documentation notebooks directly into the RAG database, which Mohammed Shaik Sahil noted provides acceptable performance for explainability.
- **Fault Prediction Methodology**: Replying to Pravin Choudhary's inquiry regarding specific fault types, Mohammed Shaik Sahil explained that the team focuses on predicting solutions rather than just identifying faults. The team deploys Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models to predict network parameters such as latency, payload, jitter, and bandwidth establishment to calculate the Estimated Time of Arrival (ETA) for network degradation. Additionally, they employ XGBoost models to determine the severity of a fault and its dependencies on other network failures, while using the RAG-driven Phi-3 model to provide qualitative explainability for these issues.
- **Quality of Service (QoS) and Service Level Agreements (SLAs)**: Mithilesh recommended integrating Quality of Service (QoS) and Service Level Agreement (SLA) parameters into the network simulation. They presented a scenario involving two customers: Customer 1 with a strict 99.9% availability SLA and Customer 2 with a flexible 90% availability requirement. Mithilesh noted that high traffic from a non-critical customer node could inadvertently degrade the traffic of a high-priority customer, and stressed that the AI must be able to identify the specific rogue node and the affected party. Pravin Choudhary supported this, explaining that Multi-Protocol Label Switching (MPLS) service providers establish a Class of Service (CoS) based on customer pricing tiers, where Class of Service 1 receives top priority on the MPLS network. They advised that the team's dashboard currently operates manually, but the AI should automate this by predicting disproportionate bandwidth consumption and proactively restricting non-critical traffic.
- **Real-World Multi-Node Modeling and Telemetry**: Pravin Choudhary provided a real-world analog using the Income Tax department's infrastructure, which comprises approximately 800 to 900 physical nodes across the country, each monitored by a central Network Operations Center (NOC) running 24/7. They explained that a typical rural edge Customer Edge (CE) router handles 2 Mbps to 3 Mbps daily, meaning a sudden surge to 15 Mbps or 16 Mbps represents an anomaly or a potential Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack that the AI must predict beforehand. Furthermore, they highlighted logical and physical layer disruptions, such as physical optical fiber damage that drops the optical budget power (TX/RX power) below safe operational thresholds (e.g., -16 dBm to -20 dBm). Pravin Choudhary emphasized that the AI must predict these threshold breaches in advance so NOC operators can make proactive mitigation decisions, while explicitly cautioning the team to avoid security-focused features and focus entirely on network implementation and uptime.
- **Hackathon Demonstration Strategy and Simulator Alternatives**: Pravin Choudhary and Mithilesh advised that the final jury will prioritize real-time predictability and live fault injection over Graphical User Interface (GUI) aesthetics or complex explainability features. To optimize this, Mithilesh questioned if the team's plug-and-play machine learning pipeline could ingest data from a GNS3 simulator instead of physical hardware. Mohammed Shaik Sahil confirmed compatibility via Prometheus data mapping and noted they are already collecting baseline data before the event. Mithilesh strongly recommended that the team construct a 15 to 20 node GNS3 simulation featuring a central headquarter and 5 to 8 attached nodes, as injecting faults and tracing device telemetry for the explainability model would be significantly cleaner on a virtualized Linux-attached GNS3 setup.
- **Hackathon Logistics and Post-Event Prospects**: Mithilesh clarified the evaluation structure, noting that they will act as mentors and judge the teams closely on the first day, but a separate, cross-domain final jury representing 15 to 16 distinct problem statements will dictate the ultimate standings on the subsequent day. When Mohammed Shaik Sahil asked about internship opportunities, Mithilesh indicated that public sector internships differ from the private sector, and Pravin Choudhary advised the team to defer the discussion until after the event to remain entirely focused on winning the cross-domain competition.
