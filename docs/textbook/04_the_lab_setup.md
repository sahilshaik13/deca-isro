# Chapter 4 — The Lab Setup

## Why we built a physical lab instead of just simulating everything in software

We could have written a pure software simulation of a network — no real
hardware at all, just numbers generated by a computer program pretending
to be a network. We deliberately did not do this. Real hardware
introduces real, physical imperfections: actual CPU processing delays,
actual queuing inside real network interfaces, actual timing quirks —
the kind of small, messy realism that a "mathematically perfect" software
simulation would accidentally smooth away. Since the whole point of DECA
is to learn to recognize *real* network behavior, we wanted our training
data to come from something as close to a real, physical network as we
could reasonably build.

**Real-life comparison:** this is the same reason a pilot trains on an
actual flight simulator cockpit with real switches and real instrument
lag, instead of just reading a textbook description of what flying feels
like — the physical, hands-on realism teaches things a description alone
cannot.

---

## The hardware: four small computers

Our whole lab is built from:

- **Three Raspberry Pi computers** — small, inexpensive, credit-card-sized
  computers, each one acting as one router in our simulated network.
- **One laptop** — the "brain" that watches everything, runs our
  experiments, and does the actual machine learning.

We nicknamed the three Raspberry Pis `station1`, `station2`, and
`station3`. This naming, and the addresses that go with it, are
explained fully in Chapter 1 (see **Hostname** and **IP address**) — this
chapter focuses on *why* they're arranged the way they are and what each
one's job is.

---

## The topology: "CE-PE-CE"

Our lab is deliberately built in the industry-standard "CE-PE-CE" shape
(Customer Edge — Provider Edge — Customer Edge — see Chapter 1 for both
terms). Here is the picture, in words first, then as a diagram:

- Picture two separate customer sites that need to talk to each other
  privately and securely, over a shared network they don't fully control
  — like two different offices of the same company, connected through
  their internet service provider's shared backbone.
- Each customer site has its own small router at the very edge of its own
  network — the "Customer Edge" (CE).
- The shared provider network has its own routers at the edge, closest to
  each customer — the "Provider Edge" (PE) routers — plus, often, one or
  more routers purely in the middle that never touch a customer directly
  — the "core."

```
                     ┌─────────────────────────────┐
                     │  Laptop (orchestrator)       │
                     │  watches everything,         │
                     │  runs the experiments,       │
                     │  trains the model            │
                     └─────────────┬───────────────┘
                                   │
         ┌─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┐
         │                         │                         │
┌────────▼────────┐      ┌─────────▼────────┐      ┌────────▼────────┐
│ station1 (PE1)  │◄────►│ station3 (CORE)  │◄────►│ station2 (PE2)  │
│ "provider edge  │      │ "the middle      │      │ "provider edge  │
│  router, site A"│      │  relay router"   │      │  router, site B"│
└────────┬────────┘      └──────────────────┘      └────────┬────────┘
         │                                                    │
┌────────▼────────┐                                  ┌────────▼────────┐
│ Customer site A │◄──── secure tunnel, over the ────►│ Customer site B│
│ ("ce-a")        │      shared provider network      │ ("ce-b")       │
└─────────────────┘                                   └─────────────────┘
```

- `station1` plays the role of **PE1** — the provider's edge router
  closest to customer site A.
- `station2` plays the role of **PE2** — the provider's edge router
  closest to customer site B.
- `station3` plays the role of the **CORE** router — it sits in the
  middle, relaying traffic between PE1 and PE2, and never directly talks
  to either customer site.
- The two "customer sites," `ce-a` and `ce-b`, are not separate physical
  computers at all — they are network namespaces (Chapter 1) living
  *inside* `station1` and `station2` respectively. This is a clever, cheap
  way to simulate "customer router" and "provider router" using only one
  physical Raspberry Pi per site, instead of needing two.

---

## Why this exact shape matters

This is not an arbitrary toy design — it is the standard, textbook way
real companies (and, very plausibly, an organization like ISRO, with
multiple sites that need private, segmented connectivity) build their
own internal networks. Building our lab in this exact shape is a
deliberate choice to make sure everything DECA learns is actually
relevant to a real network, not just relevant to our specific toy setup.
See Chapter 10 for the full discussion of what does and doesn't transfer
to a different, real network.

## Addressing: how every device gets a name and a number

Every device in the lab has two identities: a plain-language hostname
(like `station1`) and a numeric IP address (Chapter 1). Here's the full
addressing map:

| Role | Hostname | Management address (for SSH/monitoring) | Loopback / router identity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PE1 | `station1` | `192.168.50.10` | `10.1.1.1` |
| PE2 | `station2` | `192.168.50.20` | `10.1.2.1` |
| CORE | `station3` | `192.168.50.30` | `10.1.3.1` |
| Laptop (orchestrator) | `brain` | `192.168.50.1` | — |

Additionally, each simulated customer site has its own small address
range:

| Customer site | Connects to | Local link address | "Identity" address |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `ce-a` | `station1` | `10.10.1.1/30` | `10.100.1.1/32` |
| `ce-b` | `station2` | `10.10.2.1/30` | `10.100.2.1/32` |

The "identity" addresses (`10.100.1.1` and `10.100.2.1`) are the ones we
actually test end-to-end connectivity against — for example, "can `ce-a`
successfully `ping` `10.100.2.1`?" is our simplest, most fundamental
proof that the whole path — through both PE routers, through the tunnel,
through BGP, through the VRF — is genuinely working, start to finish.

## The software that turns a Raspberry Pi into a router: FRR

Each of the three stations runs a free, open-source piece of software
called **FRR** (Free Range Routing — Chapter 1), which is what actually
gives these small computers real router capabilities: OSPF, BGP, VRFs,
and MPLS-style label switching. We interact with FRR through its
command-line tool, `vtysh`.

The stations are also running several other small pieces of supporting
software, working together:

| Software | Job | Runs on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FRR | The actual routing brain (OSPF, BGP, VRF, LDP) | All three stations |
| strongSwan | Builds and maintains the IPsec encrypted tunnel between PE1 and PE2 | `station1`, `station2` |
| Telegraf | Collects measurements and makes them available for monitoring | All three stations |
| chrony | Keeps each station's clock accurately synchronized | All three stations |
| A custom "watchdog" script | Automatically notices and fixes common problems after a reboot | All three stations |

All of these are managed by **systemd** (Chapter 1), which is what
starts them automatically at boot and restarts them automatically if they
crash — a deliberate reliability choice so the lab can recover on its own
after something like a power outage, without a human needing to manually
restart every single piece by hand.

---

## The security layer: IPsec

Between `station1` and `station2`, we run a secure, encrypted tunnel
(IPsec — Chapter 1) called `deca-sdwan`, which is what actually carries
the simulated customer traffic between site A and site B securely. This
is the exact tunnel that our `tunnel_degradation` fault (Chapter 3)
targets.

---

## The private routing segment: VRFs

Both PE routers (`station1` and `station2`) run a VRF called
`vrf-mission`, which is the main, intended "customer's private routing
table" for our simulated CE-to-CE traffic. There is a second VRF,
`vrf-admin`, which exists specifically to be the *target* of our
deliberate `vrf_leakage` fault simulation (Chapter 3) — a separate,
supposedly walled-off routing context that a route should never
accidentally end up inside of.

---

## How we watch everything: the telemetry pipeline

This is the chain of tools that turns "something happening on a Raspberry
Pi" into "a number DECA's model can actually learn from." It has four
steps:

```
  ┌──────────────┐     ┌───────────┐     ┌────────────┐     ┌─────────────┐
  │ The real     │     │ Telegraf  │     │ Prometheus │     │ DECA's      │
  │ event on the │ ──► │ measures  │ ──► │ (on the    │ ──► │ feature     │
  │ router (FRR) │     │ it        │     │ laptop)    │     │ engineering │
  │              │     │ (:9273)   │     │ scrapes it │     │ (Chapter 5) │
  └──────────────┘     └───────────┘     └────────────┘     └─────────────┘
```

1. **Something happens on a station** — for example, we run our
   `vrf_leakage` fault injector, and a real route appears inside
   `vrf-admin`'s routing table.
2. **Telegraf**, running on that station, is configured to regularly run
   a small script (using a feature called `inputs.exec` — literally
   "execute this command and read its output as a measurement") that
   asks FRR, through `vtysh`, exactly how many routes are currently in
   that table. Telegraf makes that number available on port `9273`.
3. **Prometheus**, running on the laptop, regularly (every 5 seconds)
   visits ("scrapes," Chapter 1) that port on every station and pulls in
   the current numbers, storing them over time so they can be looked up
   later — "what was this number 3 minutes ago?"
4. DECA's feature engineering code (`rebuild_unified.py`, fully explained
   in Chapter 5) later reads all of this stored history back out of
   Prometheus (or from an already-exported file) and turns it into the
   smart features (slope, rolling std, etc., Chapter 2) the model
   actually learns from.

### The two custom exporters we had to build ourselves

Most of the measurements DECA reads (traffic volume, jitter, packet
loss) are common, standard measurements that Telegraf already knows how
to collect out of the box. But two of our most important signals did
not exist anywhere until we built them ourselves, specifically for this
project:

- **`vrf_route_count`** — a small shell script
  (`lab/deca-vrf-route-count.sh`) that asks FRR, through `vtysh`, exactly
  how many routes currently exist inside the `vrf-admin` VRF's BGP table.
  This is DECA's single clearest, most direct signal for the
  `vrf_leakage` fault (Chapter 3). Building this correctly required
  finding and fixing several real bugs along the way — told in full in
  Chapter 7.
- **`bgp_flap_count`** — a similar small shell script
  (`lab/deca-bgp-flap-count.sh`) that asks FRR, through `vtysh`, exactly
  how many BGP route-refresh messages have been sent and received with a
  specific neighbor — the real, live counter that actually moves every
  time our `bgp_route_flap` fault simulation runs. This is DECA's
  clearest signal for that fault, and, like the VRF exporter, its correct
  design was the result of a real investigation told in full in Chapter
  7.

Both of these small scripts needed one more piece of plumbing to work:
Telegraf runs as its own special, restricted user account (`_telegraf`),
which normally doesn't have permission to run `vtysh` commands that read
router state. We had to add a specific, narrow `sudoers` rule (Chapter 1)
that allows exactly this one script, and nothing else, to run with the
elevated permission it needs — a deliberately narrow, least-privilege
fix rather than a broad one.

## Keeping the lab healthy: diagnostics and self-healing

Because this is real hardware, real things do occasionally go wrong —
a service crashes, a clock drifts, a cable hiccups. We built two levels
of defense against this:

1. **A watchdog script**, running automatically on every station a
   minute after boot, that checks whether the important services (FRR,
   strongSwan, Telegraf) are actually running, and restarts any that
   aren't. It also re-applies a couple of specific backup routes (see
   Chapter 1, Section 5, "VRF CE static safety-net") that sometimes need
   to be reasserted after a fresh boot.
2. **A consolidated health-check tool**, `lab/deca_ops.sh`, that we can
   run at any time from the laptop to check, end-to-end, whether
   everything — routing, the tunnel, the VRF, the monitoring pipeline —
   is actually working correctly, all the way through a real `ping` test
   between the two simulated customer sites. This tool replaced five
   older, separate scripts, consolidating them into one easier-to-trust
   place (a cleanup effort described further in Chapter 11).

Here's what a fully healthy lab check confirms, step by step:

| Step | What's being checked | Plain-English meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Basic connectivity (L3) | Can every station reach every other station at all? |
| 2 | Clock sync (NTP/chrony) | Do all the stations agree on what time it is? |
| 3 | OSPF | Have the routers found each other and agreed on a map? |
| 4 | BGP | Are the routing conversations between routers up? |
| 5 | LDP | Have the label-switching agreements (Chapter 1) been made? |
| 6 | IPsec | Is exactly one secure tunnel up and established? |
| 7 | End-to-end VPN ping | Can customer site A actually reach customer site B? |
| 8 | Monitoring | Is Prometheus successfully collecting data from all three stations? |

Only when every one of these eight checks passes do we consider the lab
ready to run an experiment.
